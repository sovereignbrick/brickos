⚡ Why "Don't Trust, Verify" applies to Bitcoin companies too
The hardware wallet RNG problem, why your dice matter more than any company's firmware
The risk in brief. Every computer-generated secret depends on a random number generator (RNG). If that RNG is flawed, predictable, or, in the worst case, deliberately weakened, every wallet it ever produced is compromised. The device can look and work perfectly. You would never know.

Hardware wallets are not immune. In 2021, security researchers published findings showing that Coldcard's True Random Number Generator (TRNG) could, under certain firmware conditions, produce entropy weaker than claimed. This was not a deliberate backdoor, it was an honest engineering failure. Coldcard disclosed the issue and patched it. But the lesson is permanent: you cannot verify a closed-source TRNG by looking at the device. You can only audit what is in front of you.

Coldcard's own answer to this problem is dice rolls. Their "dice roll import" feature lets you bypass the internal TRNG entirely: you roll physical dice, enter the results, and the device mixes them in. This is exactly the philosophy behind this tool. Physical dice obey the laws of physics, not firmware. A die with no power consumption and no code cannot be silently patched.

The pattern repeats across the industry. A non-exhaustive list of reasons to verify rather than trust:
  • Firmware is code. Even open-source firmware requires you to verify the build matches the source, few users do this before every use.
  • Supply chain attacks are real. A device intercepted between factory and door can look identical to a genuine one. Counterfeit hardware wallets with altered firmware have been documented and sold on secondary markets.
  • Companies change. Acquisitions, regulatory pressure, or key-person departures can affect security posture years after you bought a device.
  • Seed generation is a one-time event. A weak seed is permanently weak, there is no patch after the fact. The risk window is exactly the moment this tool is open.
The correct model: YOU supply all unpredictability. The software does only public, auditable math. That is what this tool implements, and every line of that math is readable by pressing F12 right now, no download required, no binary to verify, no trust required.

How do dice, photo, and coin flips combine into one 24-word seed?
The three input channels are pooled together and passed through SHA-256, which condenses them into a single 256-bit secret. Because the sources are independent, a weakness in one cannot reduce what the others already contributed, layering them is always strictly safer than using any single source alone.

Step 2, Domain-separated pool
The three sources are joined with labelled prefixes before hashing:

pool = concat( "dice:" + roll_digits , image_file_bytes , "extra:" + extra_text )

The dice: and extra: labels are domain separators. Without them, dice string "12" + empty extra would produce the same pool as dice "1" + extra "2". With them, every distinct combination of inputs maps to a distinct pool.

Step 3, SHA-256 whitening (the extractor)
SHA-256 takes the entire pool and outputs exactly 256 bits. This is formally a randomness extractor. By the Leftover Hash Lemma, if the pool contains ≥ 256 bits of min-entropy, the output is computationally indistinguishable from uniform random, regardless of how unevenly distributed the inputs were. Three rules:
  • SHA-256 cannot invent entropy. Output min-entropy ≤ min(pool min-entropy, 256).
  • Adding any source with ≥ 0 entropy never hurts. A zero-entropy photo adds nothing; it cannot subtract.
  • 100 fair dice alone fill the bucket. The photo and coin flips are insurance in case the dice were biased.
Step 4, 24-word seed phrase
256 entropy bits + 8 checksum bits (first byte of SHA-256(entropy)) = 264 bits total → split into 24 groups of 11 bits → each group is an index 0–2047 into the BIP39 English list → your 24 words.

Step 5, Passphrase (separate Diceware exercise, uses the EFF list)
The passphrase is completely separate from the seed-phrase dice. You roll 5 dice per word; 6⁵ = 7776 combinations map to one word in the EFF Large Diceware list. This is why a second wordlist is needed: BIP39 has 2048 words (2¹¹), this does not divide into clean 5-die groups, and the BIP39 words were chosen for seed backup readability, not Diceware use. The EFF list has exactly 7776 words, purpose-built for physical dice, with shorter and more recognisable words. Six EFF words = ≈77 bits of passphrase entropy, recommended minimum.

1 · Dice rolls target: 100

Each balanced 6-sided die roll adds about 2.585 bits of unpredictability. 100 rolls ≈ 258 bits, just over the 256-bit maximum a 24-word seed can hold. This is the MAIN entropy source, and the only one whose amount we can measure exactly.

How do I roll the dice properly?
The goal is a fair, uninfluenced tumble every time.

Do: roll on a hard, flat surface; throw and let it tumble (a real roll, not a drop); shake in a cup or cupped hands before each throw; record every result in order; re-roll any die that lands cocked. Aim for 100 rolls.

Don't: place, slide, or spin the die; stop early because you like the numbers; reuse a "lucky" sequence; or let anyone record your rolls.

Why: the seed's entire strength is the unpredictability of these throws. A sloppy or influenced roll quietly lowers your security.
Do I need casino-grade dice?
Ideal, but not mandatory here, the tool whitens your rolls, so ordinary dice are fine with a small margin.

Cheap dice have rounded corners and drilled, painted pips; removing material unevenly makes some faces slightly likelier, a small bias, but bias is what reduces randomness. Casino / precision dice have flush, equal-weight pips, razor-sharp edges, and a transparent body (no hidden weights), so every face is as close to equally likely as manufacturing allows.

Bottom line: for meaningful value, a set of casino dice costs a few dollars and removes all doubt. Otherwise, roll a few extra (110–120) to compensate. Cheap insurance, not a strict requirement.

2 · Image entropy optional

A photo carries messy real-world noise (sensor grain, lighting, framing). We cannot measure exactly how much randomness it holds, so it is a SUPPLEMENT, never the sole source. It thickens the pool as an independent backup in case the dice were slightly biased.

No image selected
Which image gives the best entropy?
The randomness comes from invisible camera-sensor noise plus never-repeatable framing, so the best images are busy and freshly taken.

Best: a photo you take right now of a cluttered, high-detail scene (gravel, foliage, a pile of objects, fabric texture, TV static), at high resolution with low compression.

Avoid: blank walls, clear sky, solid colours (too little detail); any image that exists elsewhere (downloads, stock, screenshots, cloud-synced); and reusing a photo. If an attacker could obtain the same file, it adds no secrecy.

Important: image entropy can't be measured, so it's a supplement, a safety net on top of your dice, never a replacement.

3 · Extra entropy optional

Coin flips (H/T), extra dice, or random keystrokes. A third independent channel. Its value is independence, not quantity.

Coin flips vs. typing, what actually counts?
Coin flips are excellent and easy to verify: a fair coin gives about 1 bit per flip, so 128+ flips is meaningful on its own. Record heads/tails in order (e.g. HTHHT…).

Keyboard mashing is NOT reliably random, fingers fall into patterns. Treat typed text as a weak extra only.

Reminder: like the image, these are independent backups. Your dice remain the measurable backbone of the seed.

4 · Generate 24-word phrase

Pool = dice + image + extra → SHA-256 (a blender that concentrates randomness) → 256-bit secret → add BIP39 checksum → split into 24 standard words.

How much randomness do I actually need?
A 24-word seed holds 256 bits, the maximum. 100 fair rolls ≈ 258 bits, just over the line with a small margin (the recommended amount). The optional photo and coin flips can't push past 256; they're insurance in case a source was weaker than assumed. 256 bits is unbreakable by any computer that could ever exist, you're not trying to beat 256, only to honestly reach it.
Why hashing beats direct dice-mapping
Turning each die straight into 3 bits wastes ~0.42 bits per roll and leaves a slight bias. Blending the whole pool with SHA-256 concentrates all the real randomness into 256 clean, evenly-spread bits, the same technique Coldcard's dice import and SeedSigner use inside.

5 · Passphrase (the "25th word") optional

A passphrase is an extra secret combined with your 24 words. It is not one of the words and is never written with them. The same 24 words with a different passphrase unlock a completely different wallet. Read the three guides below before using one.

① What is the passphrase, and how strong must it be?
It is stirred together with the 24 words (via PBKDF2, 2048 rounds) to produce the actual wallet. It is the only element that adds strength beyond the seed's 256-bit ceiling, because it is never stored with the words.

Worst case: a thief finds your 24 words but not your passphrase. Your money then rests entirely on the passphrase, and it can be guessed offline at 100,000–500,000 tries/second per graphics card. The 2048 rounds barely slow this. So the passphrase must be strong on its own.

The rule that matters: length from true randomness beats "complex-looking". L3t$_in! is weak; several genuinely-random words are strong. The gold standard is Diceware, dice-picked words, each ≈ 12.9 bits.

Aim for 6 random Diceware words (~77 bits). A single dictionary word is NOT safe.

⚠️ Only counts if each word was picked by a fair die from a real list. Copying a famous phrase (e.g. "correct horse battery staple") gives near-zero real entropy.
② Master seed vs. passphrase wallet, what to save, and the "decoy" idea
Two wallets from one backup. Your 24 words alone (no passphrase) unlock ONE wallet, the standard wallet. Add a passphrase and the same 24 words unlock a completely different, hidden wallet. Nothing on the standard wallet reveals that a hidden one exists.

Under the hood: 24 words + passphrase → PBKDF2 → a 512-bit master seed → the wallet. Blank passphrase = one master seed; any passphrase = a different master seed = a different wallet. The "Preview derived master seed" button below shows this value change.

What you must save:
• Always: the 24 words (steel / paper, offline).
• If you use a passphrase: also save the passphrase, stored separately from the words and written exactly. Both are needed together to reach the hidden wallet.
• You do not save the 512-bit master seed, it is recomputed from words + passphrase each time.
• The passphrase is not inside the 24 words and cannot be recovered from them. Lose it and the hidden funds are gone.

The decoy pattern (plausible deniability): keep a small, believable amount in the standard (no-passphrase) wallet as a decoy, and the real funds in the passphrase wallet. If forced to reveal your backup, you can hand over the 24 words, opening only the decoy, while the passphrase stays secret.

Caveats: the decoy must look plausibly like your real holdings; an adversary who knows passphrases exist may keep pressing; and every different passphrase makes yet another wallet, so a single typo silently creates an empty, unrecoverable one. Always test-receive a small amount to the passphrase wallet's first address before funding it.
③ Passphrase safety rules (must-read)
1. No typo protection. The passphrase has no checksum. One wrong character silently opens a different, empty wallet with no error. Verify the first receive address before funding.
2. No recovery. Lose it and the coins are gone forever, there is no reset. Write it down; don't rely on memory alone.
3. Store it separately from the 24 words. Together in one place = no protection at all.
4. It is exact. Upper / lower case, spaces, and symbols all count and must match precisely, every time.
Build the passphrase with dice (recommended). Standard Diceware uses 5 rolls per word: five digits (each 1–6) form a code like 23541 that maps to one word in the official 7776-word EFF list. So 6 words = 30 rolls ≈ 77 bits. Roll as before, then paste the digits, every group of 5 becomes one word.
